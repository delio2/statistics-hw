Homework 2 - Cryptography and statistics in the most famous blockchains

Research: modern applications of cryptography and statistics in Bitcoin and Ethereum, with two small simulations.

1. Overview

A blockchain is a replicated ledger that strangers agree on without a central authority. Two toolboxes make this work. Cryptography provides the objects (hashes, signatures, commitments). Statistics and probability explain how the system behaves and how it can be attacked, because mining, randomness, network delay and attacker power are all random quantities.

2. Mining as a Poisson process

To mine a Bitcoin block a miner varies a nonce until SHA-256(SHA-256(header)), read as a number, is below a target T. If the hash behaves like a uniform random value, each attempt succeeds with the tiny probability p = T / 2256, independently of all the others. A long sequence of independent, rare Bernoulli trials gives:

  • the number of blocks found in a time window is approximately Poisson, with rate λ = (hash rate) · p;
  • the time between blocks is exponential with mean 1/λ, and it is memoryless: having waited 20 minutes does not make the next block closer.

The protocol aims at a mean of 10 minutes and re-adjusts the difficulty every 2016 blocks (about two weeks). The expected number of hashes per block is D · 232, where D is the difficulty, so the network hash rate can be estimated as D · 232 / 600 hashes per second. The estimate comes from a finite sample of block times: the sum of 2016 exponential intervals has relative standard deviation 1/√2016 ≈ 2.2%, which is the statistical error of each difficulty adjustment.

3. How many confirmations? The attacker catch-up probability

An attacker with a fraction q of the hash power secretly mines a competing chain, while honest miners (fraction p = 1 - q) extend the real one. After a merchant waits for z confirmations, what is the chance the attacker ever catches up? Nakamoto's white paper (section 11) models the attacker's progress as a Poisson variable with mean λ = z·q/p, and then uses the gambler's-ruin result (catch-up from a deficit of k blocks with probability (q/p)k if q < p):

P(attacker catches up) = 1 - Σk=0..z [ λk e-λ / k! ] · ( 1 - (q/p)z-k )
Confirmations zq = 10%q = 30%q = 45%

The probability falls exponentially with z when q < 1/2, which is why 6 confirmations (about an hour) became the common convention for large payments; the table reproduces the values published by Nakamoto for q = 0.1 and q = 0.3. If q ≥ 1/2 the attacker always wins eventually. Statistics also shows that "majority is not enough": Eyal and Sirer (2014) proved that a miner who withholds blocks strategically (selfish mining) can earn more than a fair share with less than half of the hash power. The threshold is 1/3 if the attacker never wins a tie, 1/4 if it wins half of the ties, and any positive share if it always wins them.

4. When randomness fails: ECDSA nonce reuse

An ECDSA signature of a message hash z with private key d uses a fresh random nonce k:

r = x(k·G) mod n        s = k-1 (z + r·d) mod n

Security needs k to be uniform on [1, n-1] and used once. If the same k signs two different messages, r is the same, and then k = (z1 - z2) / (s1 - s2) and d = (s1·k - z1) / r: two public signatures give away the private key. This is not hypothetical: Sony's PS3 signing key was recovered in 2010 because the console used a constant nonce, and in 2013 a flaw in Android's random generator produced repeated r values in Bitcoin transactions and allowed theft of funds. Even a biased nonce is dangerous: knowing only a few bits of each nonce over many signatures lets an attacker solve a lattice problem and recover the key (Howgrave-Graham and Smart, 2001; Breitner and Heninger, 2019, applied it to real Bitcoin and Ethereum signatures). This is exactly why the statistical quality of random sources is a security issue.

5. Statistics as an analysis and forensic tool

  • De-anonymisation. Addresses are pseudonyms. Heuristics such as "all inputs of one transaction belong to the same owner" let analysts cluster addresses and link them to exchanges or services (Meiklejohn et al., 2013). Privacy tools try to defeat these inferences, and the arms race is statistical.
  • Anomaly and fraud detection. Benford's law says the leading digit d of many natural datasets appears with probability log10(1 + 1/d) (30.1% for 1, 4.6% for 9). Amounts that deviate strongly can flag wash trading or fabricated data, though a deviation alone is never proof.
  • Network health. Hash-rate estimates, orphan rates and block-interval statistics are used to detect partitions, large miner outages or abnormal mining behaviour.
  • Proof of stake. In Ethereum the proposer and committees of each 12-second slot are drawn pseudo-randomly with weights given by stake, using a shared randomness beacon (RANDAO). Fairness and resistance to manipulation of this randomness is again a statistical question.
  • Zero-knowledge proofs. Systems such as Zcash use zk-SNARKs to prove a statement without revealing it; soundness is expressed as a negligible probability that a false statement is accepted.

6. Conclusion

Cryptography gives blockchains their rules, and statistics tells us how reliably they work and where they break. Mining is a Poisson process, so block times are random and confirmations are probabilistic guarantees. Signatures are only as strong as the randomness of their nonces. Many practical attacks target the probabilistic assumptions, not the mathematics of the primitives. The toy hash of Homework 1 shows the same pattern at small scale: the algebra supplies the structure, and the statistical tests check that the output behaves as the security argument assumes.
